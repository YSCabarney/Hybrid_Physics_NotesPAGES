At 4:17 PM, a sedan traveling north on 9th Street entered the intersection against a red light. The posted speed limit is 25 mph (≈ 11.2 m/s). The sedan collided with a delivery van traveling east on Avenue of the States. Skid marks from the sedan measure 18.4 meters before the point of impact.

MATH REVIEW — WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS DEBATE

All the math in this scenario uses formulas you've already practiced. Here's the full toolkit:

v = Δx / t
velocity = displacement ÷ time (direction matters)
|v| = √(vₓ² + vᵧ²)
magnitude of a diagonal velocity vector
θ = tan⁻¹(|vₓ| ÷ |vᵧ|)
direction of a velocity vector from north
Equilibrium: F_net = 0
constant velocity → no net force (Newton's 1st Law)

Sign conventions: North = positive (+), South = negative (−), East = positive (+), West = negative (−). Velocity northward is positive. Any eastward or westward component tells you the car was not moving in a straight line.

Key distinction: Speed is a scalar — just a number. Velocity is a vector — it has both size and direction. The GPS speed display is scalar. The position log data lets you calculate velocity as a vector. That difference is at the heart of this debate.

PHYSICS REFERENCE — SIGN CONVENTIONS FOR THIS SCENARIO

Use the same sign rules you've practiced: north = positive (+), south = negative (−), east = positive (+), west = negative (−). A car traveling north has positive velocity. A car that drifts east while heading north has a velocity with both a north component and an east component — making it a diagonal vector. Finding the true speed means finding the magnitude of that vector.

The Incident

MATCH REPORT — CHESTER FC vs RIVAL

What happened

At the 84th minute, a Chester FC midfielder struck the ball from a position 9 m left of center and 14 m from the goal line, shooting diagonally toward the far post. The shot applied a force of 48 N at 32° east of north (toward the goal, angled right).

At the moment of the shot, a sliding defender made contact and applied a simultaneous force of 18 N at 15° west of north (pushing the ball slightly back toward the left). The ball was stationary before the shot — it had just been passed and stopped.

After the combined forces acted, the ball traveled toward the goal line. The goal is 7.32 m wide, centered on the field. The question is whether the net force directed the ball inside the post or outside it.

The ball reached the goal line in 1.8 seconds after the forces were applied. Goal-line technology was offline. There is no definitive camera angle.

The core dispute: Team Shot argues the shot force was dominant — the net force still pointed inside the far post. Team Deflection argues the defender's force was enough to change the ball's direction outside the post. The answer is in the math — find the net force vector, its direction, and calculate where the ball actually ended up.

Sign conventions for this scenario: North = positive y (+), South = negative y (−), East = positive x (+), West = negative x (−). The shot goes north and east so both its components are positive. The deflection goes north and west — north is positive, west is negative.

Finding where the ball crossed: Once you have the net force direction (angle east of north), use geometry: the ball travels 14 m north, so east displacement = 14 × tan(angle). Add that to the starting position (9 m left of center) to find where it hit the goal line. Use v = Δx / t with the 1.8 second travel time and the total displacement to find average velocity — velocity is a vector, so you need both its size and direction.

PHYSICS REFERENCE — SIGN CONVENTIONS

North = +y. South = −y. East = +x. West = −x. For a force described as "32° east of north": the north component uses cos(32°), the east component uses sin(32°). For "15° west of north": north component uses cos(15°), but the west component is negative — use −sin(15°). Add the x-components separately from the y-components. Then use the Pythagorean theorem for magnitude and tan⁻¹ for direction.

PRESENTATION GUIDE

How to build your argument

Your team has until Friday, November 21 to build and present a physics argument. Here's exactly what that looks like.

1

Read the scenario together

Go to your debate tab (Debate 1 or Debate 2). Read the full scenario, evidence table, and diagram as a group. Make sure everyone on the team understands what actually happened — before you start calculating anything.

2

Do the math — by hand, together

Read through your evidence packet and decide as a team which data points support your argument. Then do the math — use the formulas from class: v = Δx/t, Fₓ = F cos θ, Fᵧ = F sin θ, Pythagorean theorem, tan⁻¹ for direction. You won't need every number in the evidence. Pick what matters, calculate it, and show every step: formula → numbers plugged in → answer with units. This work goes in your presentation.

Important: Every team member should be able to explain every calculation. If one person does all the math and the others just copy it, that will show during the presentation.
3

Build your presentation

You can use a slideshow (Google Slides, PowerPoint, Canva) or a poster. Either works. Your presentation must include:

  • A clear statement of your team's claim (one sentence)
  • A diagram or drawing showing the forces in your scenario
  • Your full calculation, shown step by step
  • A direct response to the other team's likely argument
  • A conclusion that connects back to the physics concepts (vectors, net force, equilibrium, velocity, displacement)
4

Split it up — everyone presents

Every member of the team must present a portion of the argument. Decide in advance who covers what:

  • Person 1: Introduce the scenario and your team's claim
  • Person 2: Walk through the math and diagram
  • Person 3: Address the other team's likely counter-argument
  • Person 4: Conclusion — why your physics proves your claim

If your team has fewer than 4 members presenting one section, combine roles. If more, split sections further. No one sits out.

5

Be ready for the jury

After your team presents, the jury teams will evaluate your argument. They may ask one question. Your team should be ready to defend your math or explain a step. The jury then votes — privately — on which team made the stronger physics argument.

Jury teams: While the other debate is happening, you are evaluating — not checking your phone. Use the rubric to take notes on both presentations. You'll vote at the end.
