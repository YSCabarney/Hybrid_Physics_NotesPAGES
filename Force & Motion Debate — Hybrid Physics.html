SIDE A — TEAM VELOCITY · Sam, Mateo, Aiden, Erik

Your position: The GPS data proves the car was not speeding. The east drift is a malfunction, not real movement.

Questions to work through (you don't have to use all of these):

  • What is the car's velocity during each 1-second interval from t = 0 to t = 4? What do those numbers tell you?
  • The GPS says position changed north by the same amount each second for four seconds. What does constant position change over equal time intervals mean for velocity? (Hint: think back to your motion graphs from Week 1.)
  • The road goes straight north. The GPS shows a 2.1 m eastward drift. How does that compare to the GPS manufacturer's stated accuracy of ±1.5 m? What could you argue from that?
  • The driver says the speed display showed 25 mph the whole time. The manufacturer notes that speed display can lag during rapid acceleration. Does that help or hurt your argument?
  • Is the 18.4 m skid mark useful to your argument? Why or why not?
  • What is a scalar vs. a vector, and which one is the GPS speed display showing? Does that distinction matter here?
SIDE B — TEAM DISPLACEMENT · Nate, Abdoulaye, Javi, Chris

Your position: The displacement data proves the car was moving faster than the speed limit at the moment of impact.

Questions to work through (you don't have to use all of these):

  • What is the car's total displacement from t = 0 to t = 6? Remember: displacement is a vector — it has both a north component and an east component. How do you find the magnitude of a diagonal vector?
  • What is the car's average velocity over the full 6 seconds? How does that compare to the speed limit?
  • Look at t = 4 to t = 5 and t = 5 to t = 6. The position changes in those intervals are different from t = 0–4. What could that mean about the car's speed near the intersection?
  • The GPS manufacturer says position logs are more reliable than the speed display, and that drift beyond ±1.5 m may indicate real lateral movement. The east drift is 2.1 m. What does that suggest?
  • The skid marks are 18.4 m long and begin 20 m south of the intersection. What does this tell you about where the driver started braking — and what does that imply about their speed?
  • Witness 3 said the car "seemed like it swerved a little." Does any of the GPS data support that? What is a vector, and how does direction factor into this?

DEBATE SCENARIO
The Robot Arm & the Bridge Model

A robotics team is testing a construction robot arm that places steel beams on a model bridge. The arm applies a diagonal pushing force to seat the beam. The beam doesn't seat correctly — it shifts and the bridge model collapses. Did the robot apply too much force, or was the bridge already unbalanced?

SIDE A — ARGUES: CORRECT FORCE, BRIDGE WAS ALREADY UNBALANCED
Team Net Force
Danny · Collin · Jayden · Will
SIDE B — ARGUES: TOO MUCH FORCE DESTROYED EQUILIBRIUM
Team Equilibrium
Timoni · Elijah · Maxi · Kenan
JURY
Sam, Mateo, Aiden, Erik & Nate, Abdoulaye, Javi, Chris — you decide who wins based on the physics.

The Evidence

ENGINEERING INCIDENT REPORT

What happened

The robot arm was programmed to place a beam with a force of 120 N at 35° below horizontal (pushing down and forward to seat the beam into its bracket). The bridge model was designed to stay in equilibrium with any downward force up to 80 N. Two support cables were each rated to hold 60 N upward.

At the moment of placement, sensor data recorded:

  • Robot arm force: 120 N at 35° below horizontal
  • Cable 1 upward tension: 58 N
  • Cable 2 upward tension: 61 N
  • Bridge beam weight: 45 N downward

The beam shifted 14 cm to the right before the collapse. The robot arm's angle sensor showed 35°, but the team that programmed it claims they set it to 20°.

The core dispute: Team Net Force argues the cables were already providing unequal tension before the robot pushed — one under its rated spec, one slightly over — and that imbalance caused the beam to shift. Team Equilibrium argues the robot's 35° angle produced a vertical force component that pushed the beam beyond its design limit, destroying equilibrium regardless of what the cables were doing.

Additional Evidence — Available to Both Teams

The following data was collected from sensors, the engineering team, and the robotics company. Not all of it supports both sides equally. Decide what to use, what to leave out, and what to push back on.

ROBOT ARM SENSOR LOG

  • Programmed force: 120 N
  • Programmed angle: 20° below horizontal (per robotics team log)
  • Angle recorded by arm sensor at time of placement: 35° below horizontal
  • Force recorded by arm sensor at time of placement: 120 N (consistent with programming)
  • Arm sensor calibration: last verified 3 weeks before incident; manufacturer recommends weekly calibration for precision placement
  • Beam shift recorded: 14 cm to the right before collapse

BRIDGE DESIGN SPECIFICATIONS

  • Maximum downward force before equilibrium fails: 80 N
  • Each support cable rated for: 60 N upward (combined max: 120 N)
  • Bridge beam weight: 45 N downward
  • Design assumes: force applied at 20° or less below horizontal
  • Design note: "Unequal cable tension exceeding 5 N difference may cause lateral beam shift prior to full collapse."

CABLE SENSOR READINGS — AT MOMENT OF PLACEMENT

  • Cable 1 (left side): 58 N upward
  • Cable 2 (right side): 61 N upward
  • Both cables within individual rating (60 N each)
  • Pre-placement baseline reading (before robot arm engaged): Cable 1: 22.5 N, Cable 2: 22.5 N (beam weight only, balanced)

ROBOTICS TEAM STATEMENT

"We programmed 20°. We don't know why the sensor logged 35°. The arm sensor is overdue for calibration — it may have drifted. The force was correct at 120 N. If the angle was really 35°, that's a hardware failure, not a programming error. And even at 35°, we calculated the net downward force and it still didn't exceed the design limit."

BRIDGE ENGINEERING TEAM STATEMENT

"The bridge was in perfect equilibrium before the arm engaged — our pre-placement sensors confirm equal cable tension at 22.5 N each. The 80 N design limit refers to vertical force on the beam, not total force. The robot applied a diagonal force at 35°, which means only part of it was vertical — but that vertical part is what matters for our design limit. The cables were fine. The angle was the problem."

Your Team's Evidence Packet

Use these prompts to build your argument. You don't have to answer every question — pick the ones that help your case and be ready to explain why.

SIDE A — TEAM NET FORCE · Danny, Collin, Jayden, Will

Your position: The robot applied the correct force. The bridge collapsed because the cables were providing unequal tension — the structure was already unbalanced.

Questions to work through (pick what helps your argument):

  • The robot applied 120 N at 35° below horizontal. A diagonal force has two components. Which component — horizontal or vertical — is the one that actually pushes down on the beam? How would you calculate it?
  • The bridge design limit is 80 N of downward force. The beam itself weighs 45 N. What is the total downward force on the beam, combining the beam weight and the robot's vertical component? Does it exceed 80 N?
  • Cable 1 read 58 N and Cable 2 read 61 N. That's a 3 N difference. The design notes say a difference exceeding 5 N can cause lateral shift. Is 3 N over or under that threshold? What does that tell you?
  • Before the robot arm engaged, cables were at 22.5 N each — perfectly balanced. After the arm engaged, they read differently. What caused the cable tension to change? Was it the cable itself or the force applied?
  • The robotics team says they programmed 20°. If the actual angle was 35° due to a sensor malfunction, who is responsible for that — and does it change whether the force exceeded the design limit?
  • What does it mean for an object to be in equilibrium? Was the beam in equilibrium before the robot arm moved? Use the pre-placement cable readings to support your answer.
SIDE B — TEAM EQUILIBRIUM · Timoni, Elijah, Maxi, Kenan

Your position: The robot arm applied too much downward force by using the wrong angle. That force destroyed the bridge's equilibrium. The cables were not the problem.

Questions to work through (pick what helps your argument):

  • The robot applied 120 N at 35° below horizontal. What formula gives you the vertical (downward) component of a diagonal force? Calculate it. How does it compare to the 80 N design limit?
  • The bridge was designed for a maximum angle of 20°. If the arm had used 20° (as programmed), what would the vertical component have been? Would the bridge have stayed in equilibrium?
  • The pre-placement cable readings showed perfect balance — 22.5 N each. That means the cables were working correctly before the arm moved. What does that tell you about whether the cables caused the collapse?
  • The bridge design notes say the 80 N limit refers to vertical force on the beam. The robot arm force is diagonal — only part of it acts vertically. How do you separate the vertical component from the full 120 N? Which trig function do you use?
  • The robotics team says "even at 35°, the net downward force didn't exceed the design limit." Do you agree? What calculation would you need to do to check that claim — and what numbers would you plug in?
  • What does equilibrium mean, and what has to be true about the net force for a structure to be in equilibrium? Use that definition to frame your argument.

PRESENTATION GUIDE

How to build your argument

Your team has until Friday, November 21 to build and present a physics argument. Here's exactly what that looks like.

1

Read the scenario together

Go to your debate tab (Debate 1 or Debate 2). Read the full scenario, evidence table, and diagram as a group. Make sure everyone on the team understands what actually happened — before you start calculating anything.

2

Do the math — by hand, together

Read through your evidence packet and decide as a team which data points support your argument. Then do the math — use the formulas from class: v = Δx/t, Fₓ = F cos θ, Fᵧ = F sin θ, Pythagorean theorem, tan⁻¹ for direction. You won't need every number in the evidence. Pick what matters, calculate it, and show every step: formula → numbers plugged in → answer with units. This work goes in your presentation.

Important: Every team member should be able to explain every calculation. If one person does all the math and the others just copy it, that will show during the presentation.
3

Build your presentation

You can use a slideshow (Google Slides, PowerPoint, Canva) or a poster. Either works. Your presentation must include:

  • A clear statement of your team's claim (one sentence)
  • A diagram or drawing showing the forces in your scenario
  • Your full calculation, shown step by step
  • A direct response to the other team's likely argument
  • A conclusion that connects back to the physics concepts (vectors, net force, equilibrium, velocity, displacement)
4

Split it up — everyone presents

Every member of the team must present a portion of the argument. Decide in advance who covers what:

  • Person 1: Introduce the scenario and your team's claim
  • Person 2: Walk through the math and diagram
  • Person 3: Address the other team's likely counter-argument
  • Person 4: Conclusion — why your physics proves your claim

If your team has fewer than 4 members presenting one section, combine roles. If more, split sections further. No one sits out.

5

Be ready for the jury

After your team presents, the jury teams will evaluate your argument. They may ask one question. Your team should be ready to defend your math or explain a step. The jury then votes — privately — on which team made the stronger physics argument.

Jury teams: While the other debate is happening, you are evaluating — not checking your phone. Use the rubric to take notes on both presentations. You'll vote at the end.

Presentation day schedule

~5 min Setup — teams take their spots, jury gets rubric sheets
~8 min Debate 1 — Team Velocity presents, then Team Displacement presents
~2 min Jury questions, then vote
~8 min Debate 2 — Team Net Force presents, then Team Equilibrium presents
~2 min Jury questions, then vote
