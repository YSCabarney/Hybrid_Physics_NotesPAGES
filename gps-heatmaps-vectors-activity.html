The Tech on Their Backs

⏱ 2 min

Every player in a professional match wears a small GPS tracker tucked into a vest between their shoulder blades. It records their exact position on the field 10–20 times every second — way more often than your video's 30 frames per second, but it's the same basic idea you've been using all lab: position, sampled over time.

🔁

Same math, bigger budget. Your phone gave you position at six cones. Their GPS chip gives an analyst position at thousands of points across 90 minutes. Feed those points through the same formulas you've used in Parts A–C, and you get a player's speed, their velocity at any moment, and their total distance covered.

Notice this is exactly Part C of your lab — a path that isn't straight, a distance that's bigger than the displacement, and a direction that only the displacement can give you. The only thing that changed is who's running: you on the field, or a professional on TV.

Where the Heat Map Comes From

⏱ 2 min

A heat map is just thousands of those GPS position points, stacked on top of each other and colored by how often the player stood there. It's the broadcast graphic's way of showing where someone spent their match.

A winger's heat map

Bright red/orange = lots of GPS pings recorded there. This winger clearly lived on the right wing, near the attacking corner — exactly where you'd expect from the counterattack data above.

⚠

Common mix-up: a heat map shows where a player was — it's built from distance-style data (every point they touched), not displacement. A player can have a huge, colorful heat map covering the whole pitch and still finish the match only a few meters from their starting spot. Distance and displacement almost never match — which is the whole point of Part C.
