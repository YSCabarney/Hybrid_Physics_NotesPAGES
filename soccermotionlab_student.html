The Question We Are Investigating

Investigating question: When a soccer ball travels 25 m down the field, how does the way it is set in motion — a steady dribble versus a single kick that is left to roll — affect how its speed changes along the way?

Independent variable
(what we change)		How the ball is set in motion: steady dribble (Part A) vs. one free-rolling kick (Part B).
Dependent variable
(what we measure)		The ball’s speed in each 5 m interval (m/s), found from your video.
Controlled variables
(kept the same)		The 25 m course and 5 m marker spacing; the same ball; the same field and surface; the same camera position; the same partner moving the ball; as level a strip of ground as you can find.

Your prediction (one sentence, written before you collect data): what do you think each ball’s speed will do over the 25 m?

Materials

  • A soccer field you can use when no game or practice is running
  • 2 soccer balls
  • 1 measuring tape, at least 30 m / 100 ft (a long trundle wheel also works)
  • 6 cones, water bottles, or shoes to use as markers
  • 2 phones — one for video, one for a stopwatch
  • This packet, a pen, a straightedge/ruler, and a protractor (for Part C)
  • Optional: a free video-analysis app (e.g., Vernier Video Physics) or your phone’s frame-by-frame view in Photos
Safety — read before you go
  • Use the field only when it is clear. Keep away from other people using the space.
  • The person filming and timing stands well outside the ball’s path, never in the kicking lane.
  • Never kick a ball toward people, roads, parking lots, goals with people in them, or windows.
  • Wear athletic shoes. Watch your footing on wet grass. Bring water; don’t do this in extreme heat.
  • Film in landscape. Brace the phone or set it on a bag — do not walk backward while staring at a screen.

How to film so you can get data from the video

  1. Landscape orientation. Stand 15–20 m back from the course, square to it, so all six markers and the whole run stay in frame the entire time.
  2. Keep the camera still. A moving camera ruins your timing.
  3. Use normal video (most phones record 30 frames per second) or slow–motion for the fast Part B kick.
  4. Start recording before the ball moves. Call “3–2–1–go.”
  5. Get time from the video one of two ways:
    • Frame counting: step through the clip frame by frame. time = frame number ÷ frames per second. At 30 fps each frame ≈ 0.033 s.
    • On–screen timer: place the stopwatch phone (running, big digits) next to the 0 m marker so it is in the video, and read it as the ball passes each marker.
  6. Define t = 0 as the frame where the ball first crosses the 0 m marker.
  7. For each marker, scrub to the frame where the center of the ball lines up with it. Record that time.
  8. Do 2 trials for Parts A and B. You will average them.

Part A — Steady Dribble: Position, Speed & the Slope of a Graph

Concepts: variables, speed & average speed, position–time graph, slope = velocity, constant speed (01.01, 01.05, 01.07, 01.08)

Procedure

  1. Mover places a ball at the 0 m marker.
  2. On “go,” Mover dribbles the ball in a straight line to the 25 m marker at the most constant pace they can — try not to speed up or slow down.
  3. Recorder films the entire run with the camera held still (see Diagram 1).
  4. Repeat for Trial 2.
  5. From each video, record the time the ball crosses each marker in Table A.
  6. Average the two trials at each position.
  7. For each 5 m interval, compute interval speed = 5 m ÷ (time to cross that interval).
  8. Compute overall average speed = 25 m ÷ (total average time) = m/s.

Part C — Drawing the Trip as Arrows: a First Look at Vectors

Concepts: distance vs. displacement, average speed vs. average velocity (01.10) + an introduction to vectors and resultants (next unit)

This part previews our next unit. Vectors will be new to you — that’s expected. Follow the steps slowly, use a ruler, and don’t worry about being perfectly precise. The goal is to see the idea.

What is a vector?

A vector is a quantity that has both a size (magnitude) and a direction. We draw a vector as an arrow:

  • the length of the arrow shows the magnitude, drawn to a chosen scale (like a map scale), and
  • the way the arrow points shows the direction.

Displacement is a vector: “21 m toward the northeast” is a size and a direction. Plain distance (“45 m of walking”) is only a size, so it is not a vector.

How to add vectors: “tip to tail”

  1. Pick a starting point (the origin). Draw the first arrow from there, to scale, pointing the right way. The start of an arrow is its tail; the arrowhead end is its tip.
  2. Start the next arrow exactly at the tip of the one before it. Draw it to scale in its own direction.
  3. Repeat for every vector, each new arrow beginning at the previous arrow’s tip.
  4. Draw one final arrow from the tail of the very first vector to the tip of the very last vector. That arrow is the resultant — it shows your overall displacement from start to finish.

Follow-along mini–example: walk 4 m East, then 3 m North

Tip to tail, the resultant arrow reaches straight from the start to the end. With a ruler it measures 5 squares = 5 m, pointing up and to the right. Math check: √(4² + 3²) = √25 = 5. Same answer.

Now do your own path (Grid C)

Scale for Grid C: 1 square = 2 m. So your three legs become:

  • Leg 1 — 15 m East → 15 ÷ 2 = 7½ squares to the right
  • Leg 2 — 20 m North → 10 squares up
  • Leg 3 — 10 m West → 5 squares to the left
  1. Put a dot on the marked origin A. With a ruler, draw Leg 1 as a straight arrow 7½ squares to the right. Add an arrowhead; label the tip B.
  2. Starting at B, draw Leg 2 as an arrow 10 squares straight up. Arrowhead; label the tip C.
  3. Starting at C, draw Leg 3 as an arrow 5 squares straight left. Arrowhead; label the tip D.
  4. Using a different color, draw the resultant: one straight arrow from A to D.
  5. Measure the resultant. Ruler: how many squares long is it? × 2 m = m. Protractor: put it at A with 0° pointing East (right); measure the angle up to your resultant arrow = °.
Another way to get the same answer (math check). Because every turn in your path is a square (90°) corner:
  • East–West: you went 15 m east and 10 m west, so the net is 15 − 10 = m east.
  • North–South: you went 20 m north, so the net is m north.
  • Those two make a right triangle with the resultant as the long side: resultant = √(east² + north²) = √( ² + ² ) = m
  • direction = tan⁻¹( north ÷ east ) = tan⁻¹( ) = ° north of east
Your ruler-and-protractor answer should land within a couple of meters and a few degrees of this. If it is far off, re-check your scale (7½, 10, 5 squares) and that each arrow started at the previous arrow’s tip.

Discussion Questions

Answer in complete sentences. Show your work on any calculation. Use your data.

1. What does the slope of your Part A position–time graph represent? Give its units, and compare it to the average speed you found from total distance ÷ total time.
2. Compare your interval speeds for Part A and Part B. Was the Part A ball’s speed essentially constant? Did the Part B ball’s speed change? Use specific numbers from both tables, and state whether your prediction for the investigating question was supported.
3. In Part C, explain the difference between the total distance the ball traveled and its displacement. Which one did you use to find average velocity, and why?
4. You added three arrows tip–to–tail to find the resultant. In your own words, describe where each arrow starts and where the resultant arrow goes. Why can you not just add 15 + 20 + 10 to get the displacement?
5. Compare your Part C average speed and average velocity. Why are they different numbers? Describe a path for which they would be equal.
6. In Part B, what happened to the ball’s speed over each 5 m interval? Name the force responsible and say which direction it points relative to the ball’s motion.
7. Based on Part B, define acceleration in your own words. If you plotted the ball’s speed vs. time for Part B, what would the slope of that graph tell you, and would it be positive or negative here?
8. A velocity can change through a change in speed, a change in direction, or both. Point to one moment in this lab where velocity changed because of direction only, and one where it changed because of speed only.
9. Your partner filmed Part A from the sideline, standing still. Suppose instead they had jogged alongside the ball at exactly the ball’s speed while filming. What would the ball’s motion look like in that video? Answer using the idea of a frame of reference.
10. Identify two sources of measurement error in your video analysis (for example, judging exactly which frame the ball crosses a marker). For each, say whether it would tend to make your calculated speed too high or too low, and give one change to your procedure that would reduce it.
